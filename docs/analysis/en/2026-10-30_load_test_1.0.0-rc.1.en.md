# Load test of 1.0.0-rc.1 (October 30, 2026)

*French version: [2026-10-30_test_de_charge_1.0.0-rc.1.fr.md](../fr/2026-10-30_test_de_charge_1.0.0-rc.1.fr.md)*

The release checklist of 1.0.0-rc.1 carried one line nobody had measured: *a few hundred targets, tens of
thousands of findings*. This is that measurement: a seeded estate of 300 targets and 75,000 issues, a
scripted mix of what people, pipelines and agents call, four levels of concurrency, before and after
three fixes. The harness is in [`load/`](../../../load/README.md) and reruns the whole of it.

## In short

- **Before the fixes the control plane did not hold an estate of this size, at any level.** Two gate
  calls a second from pipelines were enough: each verdict read the whole component inventory (1.65 s of
  MySQL, 390,000 rows), the ten database connections were held by the gate, and every other route queued
  behind it. With one person at work, a sign-in took 47 s at p95 and the dashboard 25 s; with ten, a
  quarter of the sign-ins and a third of the agents' claims timed out.
- **Three defects, fixed in this release** (V96 to V98): the gate and the dashboards read the inventory
  instead of an index; ten queries read every stored SBOM to test whether a scan had one; the scan
  history sorted the table with its SBOMs. Unloaded, the gate went from 1.85 s to 45 ms, the dashboard
  from 2.9 s to 0.5 s, the licence summary from 1.2 s to 20 ms.
- **After the fixes, ten very active people** (one screen a second each, about eleven requests a second
  with the pipelines and the agents) **are served with no error and every screen but four under 0.6 s at
  p95**, the gate under 0.1 s. The four are the dashboard (2.1 s), the inventory search (1.0 s), the OWASP
  grid (1.0 s) and the licence inventory (0.9 s); a scan's result is ingested in 1.8 s at p95.
- **At fifty such people the database's CPU is the limit** — 330 % of the four cores it had — and every
  route queues for a connection: p95 between 1.4 s and 12 s, still with no error. At a hundred, the
  1.5 GiB heap reached full collections. What uses that CPU is known and listed below, with what each
  would take; none was a focused fix.

## The scenario

Four scenarios ran together for 90 s at each level (`load/k6/scenario.js`):

- **People**, a closed model: `N` virtual users, each opening a screen, thinking 0.5 to 1.5 s, opening the
  next. Two thirds administrators or leads — the issue list (first page, filtered, searched, a deep page),
  an issue, a triage, the dashboard, trends, security overview, scorecards, posture analytics, inventory
  search, licences, OWASP grid, remediation debt, scans, targets, a target's CSV export; one third
  restricted readers — their issue list, filtered, an issue, the dashboard, their repositories. **One
  screen a second is far busier than a person**: ten such users are closer to a hundred people at work.
- **Pipelines**, an open model: two gate calls a second (`POST /api/v1/gate`), with keys restricted to
  their repository.
- **Sign-ins**: one every two seconds, each followed by a sign-out.
- **Scans**: one asked for every five seconds; four agents long-poll (`wait=5`), claim and hand back a
  420 KB result (1,600 components, 200 vulnerabilities), which the control plane ingests.

## What broke, and what was fixed

### The gate read the whole inventory at every verdict (V96)

A verdict carries the ecosystems of its target that no installed rule covers, and to answer for one
target the gate asked the estate: `select distinct repo_id, container_id, purl from t_component` —
391,844 rows and 1.65 s of MySQL a call. The dashboard and the security overview asked the same of every
target at every call; the OWASP grid and the rule-set coverage asked it without targets (30,000 rows).
The comment on the query said distinctness kept the answer proportional to the ecosystems; a purl carries
a name and a version, so it did not.

Rule coverage reads only a purl's type, the prefix up to its first slash. V96 stores it as
`t_component.purl_type`, a generated column the engine keeps through every write, indexed with the
component's target; the gate reads its own target's on the index's prefix, the estate's answer is a loose
index scan (1,500 entries rather than a million, 350 → 40 ms). Gate 1.85 s → 45 ms; dashboard
2.9 s → 0.5 s; security overview 2.0 s → 0.17 s.

### Ten queries read every SBOM to ask whether a scan had one (V98)

`sbom is null` and `sbom is not null` appeared in ten queries of the scans: the licence tallies' census,
the inventory's scans, each target's newest completed scan. MySQL stores a long `longtext` off the row and
reads it back to test it for null — every stored SBOM, 500 ms a statement against 0.3 ms for a count by
status over the same rows. The licence summary, the licence inventory, the scorecards and the posture
analytics ran two each. V98 adds `t_scan.has_sbom`, a generated column the queries read instead. Licence
summary 1.2 s → 20 ms, scorecards 1.35 s → 0.2 s, posture analytics 1.4 s → 0.27 s. The cost removed grew
with the SBOMs kept; real SBOMs are larger than this estate's 340 KB.

### The scan history sorted the table with its SBOMs (V97)

The scans list and the dashboard's recent scans read `t_scan` newest first with a limit, and no index led
with that order. MySQL sorted the whole table, and its sort carries the selected columns — the SBOM among
them. V97 adds an index on `(created_at, id)`: the statement went from 493 to 5 ms, the route from 630 to
110 ms.

Each fix has a test that fails without it (mutation-checked); the migrations ran on the seeded estate in
10 s together, the two generated columns rebuilding their tables.

## What was found and not fixed

Each of these is larger than a focused fix, and is what to work on next for an estate of this size.

1. **The issue list sorts the open backlog at every page.** The default order is a `case` over the
   severity, which no index can serve: every page sorts the 60,000 open issues and their selected
   columns. 135 ms unloaded; 150 ms a call in MySQL at fifty users, the largest single share of the
   database's time (180 s of 90). A stored severity rank, indexed with the state and the last-seen
   time, would make a page an index walk — at the price of the rank being written in a migration as well
   as in `Severity`, which `IssueOrdering` exists to avoid.
2. **The dashboard reads and returns the whole failing posture.** Each call reads every open issue
   (60,000 rows) to grade every target, counts the backlog by severity in six statements of 35 to 50 ms
   each, and answers 1.7 MB: every failing target with every violation. 0.45 s unloaded, 2.1 s at p95
   with ten users, and the likeliest reason the heap filled at a hundred. An index on
   `t_issue (state, severity, type, triage_status)` takes a severity count from 47 to 2.5 ms (measured);
   the payload and the per-call grading would need a paged or summarised response, which is a contract
   change.
3. **The inventory search scans every component row.** `name like '%…%'` cannot use an index: 1.8 s of
   MySQL a call under load, a million rows read. A search by prefix, or an n-gram index, is a design
   decision about what the search promises.
4. **The licence inventory costs 0.6 s of the control plane's CPU a call**, served from memory since
   this release, on 300 targets of three scans and a thousand components each. It grows with the
   inventory kept, not with the users.
5. **The scan history still reads the SBOM of each scan it lists**: the list loads entities, and an
   entity carries its document — 50 × 340 KB a page, 415 ms a call in MySQL at fifty users. A projection
   without the payloads would remove it; the same holds wherever a scan entity is loaded for its status.
6. **Sign-in costs a database connection like any route**, so it queued with the rest: 8 s at p95 at
   fifty users. The password hash is not the cost (58 ms unloaded).

## Sizing

What this run supports, for an estate of about 300 targets and 60,000 open issues; the page
[Sizing](../../../docs-site/administration/sizing.md) carries the same for operators.

- **Database CPU decides how many people can work at once.** Four cores served ten very active users
  (about a hundred people at a human pace) with the slowest screen at 2 s p95, and were saturated at
  fifty. Give MySQL four cores for a few hundred targets and a team of a hundred, eight beyond, and raise
  the pool only with the cores: Hikari's ten connections were never the limit here.
- **Heap**: 1.5 GiB held fifty users; at a hundred, it filled. 2 GiB (the chart's limit) for a team, 3
  to 4 GiB of heap — a 4 to 5 GiB container limit — for estates of this size with many people at once.
- **Database size**: 875 MB for this estate. Per scan kept, its SBOM (340 KB here; a real Syft document
  is often 1 to 5 MB) and about 0.3 KB per component row with its indexes; per issue about 1 KB, per
  finding 0.35 KB. The retention purge bounds the SBOMs, not the component rows, which are kept for the
  inventory.
- **Ingestion**: four agents handed back 300 results of 420 KB in 70 s — about 4 results a second, 0.8 s
  each, about 15,000 an hour — without slowing the screens at ten users.
- **The buffer pool**: the composition leaves MySQL's 128 MiB default, and this estate's working set was
  larger; the statements above were CPU-bound rather than disk-bound on this machine, but on a server
  with slower storage give InnoDB a buffer pool near the data size (`--innodb-buffer-pool-size`).

## A nightly smoke test (proposed, not wired)

The full run takes half an hour and a machine to itself, which CI should not pay on every push. A smoke
version could run in `nightly.yml` in about five minutes: seed 30 targets and two rounds
(`seed.py --targets 30 --rounds 2`, about a minute), one level of ten users for 60 s, and k6 thresholds
that fail the job — every route's error rate at zero, the gate under 300 ms and the dashboard under 2 s at
p95. On a runner the numbers are not comparable with this report's, so the thresholds would catch a
return of the defects above (seconds where milliseconds are expected), not a drift of tens of percent.

## Reproducing

```bash
(cd vectispire-java && ./gradlew :vectispire-core:bootJar -x test)
load/env.sh up
python3 load/seed/seed.py --governor-password "$(cat load/.state/bootstrap_password)"
load/env.sh snapshot && load/env.sh app
load/probe.sh
LEVELS="1 10 50 100" DURATION=90s load/run.sh mylabel
python3 load/report.py load/results/<run>
load/env.sh down
```
